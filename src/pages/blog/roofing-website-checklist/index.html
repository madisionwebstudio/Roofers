Roofing website checklist: 25 things your site needs

Use this to grade your current site, or to check any agency's work — including ours. Each item takes a homeowner one step closer to calling you.

{{img name="crew-pair" alt="Two roofers in harnesses working together on a shingle roof" sizes="100vw"}}

Most homeowners only shop for a roofer a few times in their life, usually under pressure: a leak over the bedroom, shingles in the yard after a storm, an insurance adjuster on the calendar. They don't browse. They land on your site, decide in seconds whether you look legitimate, and either call or hit the back button.

That's what this checklist is built around. It's not about making a site “pretty.” It's about removing every reason a homeowner might have to leave without calling.

We've grouped the 25 items into five sections. Go through them with your own site open on your phone — that's how most of your visitors will see it.

The first screen

The “first screen” is everything a visitor sees before they scroll. On a phone, that's not much. These five things need to be in it.

1. A headline that says what you do and where

“Quality you can trust” tells a homeowner nothing. “Roof replacement and storm repair in San Antonio” tells them they're in the right place. Put the service and the area in the biggest text on the page.

2. A phone number you can tap

On mobile, your number should be a tap-to-call link, not just text. Test it: open your site on your phone and tap the number. If the dialer doesn't open, you're losing calls.

3. One clear button

“Get a free inspection” or “Get a quote.” Pick one main action and make it the brightest thing on the page. Three different buttons competing for attention usually means none get clicked.

4. A real photo

Your crew, your trucks, a roof you actually finished. Stock photos of smiling models in hard hats make a site look like a template — homeowners notice. If you don't have good photos yet, take some on your next job in good light.

5. A trust line under the button

A short row like “Licensed & insured · Free inspections · Financing available.” Only list things that are true for your company, and be ready to back them up.

Trust

Roofing has a reputation problem after every big storm, when out-of-town crews knock on doors. Your site's job is to prove you're local, legitimate and still going to be around when the warranty matters.

6. Your license and insurance details

Requirements vary by state — some states license roofers, some don't. Whatever applies to you, show it, ideally with a license number homeowners can look up.

7. Real Google reviews

Show your actual Google reviews, pulled from your profile, with a link to see them all. Never write reviews yourself, copy them from somewhere else or pay for them — the FTC finalized a rule banning fake reviews and testimonials in 2024 (FTC), and Google removes them.

8. Manufacturer certifications — only if you have them

If you're certified by a shingle manufacturer, show it, because it can give the homeowner access to better warranties. If you're not, don't use their logos.

9. A real address or service-area statement

If you have an office or yard customers can visit, show the address. If you work from home, say which towns you serve instead. Don't invent an office.

10. An About page with faces

People hire people. A photo of the owner and crew, how long you've been in business, and why you do it this way will do more for you than any slogan.

Pages

Each page should answer one question a homeowner (or Google) has. A one-page site can't rank for “roof repair,” “hail damage” and “roof replacement” at once — separate pages can.

11. Roof replacement

Usually your biggest job and the one homeowners research most. Cover materials you install, how long a typical job takes, what's in your warranty, and financing.

12. Roof repair

Shorter and more urgent. Leaks, missing shingles, flashing. Make the phone number even more prominent here.

13. Storm and hail damage

What to look for after a storm, what to photograph, and why a professional inspection matters. This page earns trust before the homeowner even calls.

14. Insurance claims help

Explain, in plain words, how you work with the homeowner's insurance. Be careful with promises: rules on what a contractor may say or do around claims vary by state.

15. Projects gallery

Before and after photos, labeled with the town and the work done. Update it often — it's proof that you're busy and local.

16. Service areas

List the towns and suburbs you cover. If one area is especially important to you, it can have its own page — but only if you can write something genuinely specific about working there.

Turning visits into calls

17. A short quote form

Name, phone, address, what's going on. That's it. Every extra field gives someone a reason to quit. You can ask the rest on the phone.

18. A sticky call button on mobile

A bar at the bottom of the screen with “Call” and “Free inspection” that follows the visitor as they scroll. Simple, and it works.

19. A financing section

A new roof is a big, often unplanned expense. If you offer financing, say so clearly and link to your partner's application. If you don't, consider it.

20. A thank-you page that says what happens next

After someone submits the form, tell them when you'll call and what to have ready. It reduces no-shows and gives you a clean way to track conversions in Google Analytics and Google Ads.

Speed, SEO and the boring stuff

None of this shows up in a design mockup. All of it affects whether you get found and whether the site works when it matters.

21. Fast on a phone

Test your site with Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool. Slow pages lose visitors: the longer a page takes to load on a phone, the more people give up and leave. Big uncompressed photos and heavy page builders are the usual culprits.

22. Unique titles and descriptions on every page

The title is the blue link people see on Google. “Home” or “Untitled” is a wasted chance. Each page should have its own title with the service and area.

23. Schema markup

Structured data that tells Google, in its own language, that you're a roofing business, where you work and how to contact you. Homeowners never see it; search engines read it.

24. Google Search Console and Analytics

Search Console shows which searches you appear for and flags problems. Analytics shows what visitors do. Both are free. Make sure they're set up in your name, not your web designer's.

25. You own it

Your domain should be registered in your name. You should have admin access to your Google Business Profile, Analytics and any ad accounts. If a provider controls these, leaving them gets painful. Ask before you sign anything.

Five mistakes that quietly cost roofers calls

These come up again and again when we review roofing sites. None of them look dramatic, which is exactly why they survive for years.

The phone number is an image

Some templates put the phone number inside a header graphic. It looks fine, but it can't be tapped, copied or read by Google. Make it real text, and make it a link.

The contact form goes nowhere

Forms break silently: an old email address, a full inbox, a spam filter. Send yourself a test from your own site once a month. If you've never done it, do it today — and check the spam folder too.

An outdated storm page

A storm page from three seasons ago, with an old date at the top, tells homeowners nobody's home. Either keep it current or write it so it doesn't go stale.

Pop-ups on mobile

A chat bubble, a cookie bar and a “get 10% off” pop-up on a 6-inch screen can cover the whole page. Google also warns against intrusive pop-ups on mobile (Google Search Central). Keep one, at most, and make it easy to close.

Nobody can edit it

If changing your hours means emailing a designer who replies in two weeks, the site will drift out of date. Whatever you build, make sure someone in your office can update basic details in minutes.

A 10-minute test you can do today

Grab your phone, switch off Wi-Fi so you're on mobile data, and pretend you're a homeowner who just found water stains on the ceiling.

  1. Search Google for “roof repair” plus your town. Can you find yourself — in the map or the results?
  2. Tap through to your site. Count the seconds until you can read the headline.
  3. Without scrolling, can you tell what you do, where, and how to call?
  4. Tap the phone number. Does the dialer open?
  5. Fill in the quote form with your own details. Did the email arrive? How long did it take?
  6. Find a photo of a job you actually did, and a real review.

If any step frustrated you, it frustrates homeowners too — and they don't have a reason to push through it.

How did your site score?

If you ticked 20 or more, you're in good shape — focus on reviews and getting found. If you ticked fewer than 15, the website itself is probably costing you calls, and no amount of advertising will fully fix that.
