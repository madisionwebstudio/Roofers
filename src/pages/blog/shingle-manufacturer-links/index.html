Are you sending customers to shingle manufacturer websites?

Those certification logos build trust — until a homeowner clicks one and lands on a page listing your competitors. Here is how to fix it.

{{img name="home-new-roof" alt="House with a newly installed dark shingle roof" sizes="100vw"}}

Look at almost any roofing website and you'll find a row of manufacturer logos — shingle brands, certification badges, warranty programs. That's good: certifications build trust. But click those logos, and many of them send the homeowner straight to the manufacturer's website.

Once they're there, they see the manufacturer's own “find a contractor” tool, a list of other certified roofers in their area, and a dozen reasons to keep browsing. You paid to get that homeowner onto your site — and a logo just sent them away.

How roofing sites leak visitors

The most common leaks we see on roofing websites:

  • Clickable manufacturer badges in the header, footer or trust bar that link to the brand's homepage.
  • “Learn more about our shingles” buttons that open the manufacturer's product pages instead of your own.
  • Warranty links that go to the manufacturer's warranty site rather than a page explaining what you offer.
  • Embedded color visualizers or brochures hosted on another site, with no way back.
  • Links that open in the same tab, so the back button is the only way home.

Each one is small. Together they quietly cost calls.

Why it matters more than it looks

Manufacturer sites help homeowners compare contractors

Many shingle manufacturers run contractor-locator tools that list several certified roofers near the homeowner. That's useful for the manufacturer — and it means a homeowner who clicks your badge can end up looking at your competitors.

You lose the visitor at the worst moment

People click a certification badge when they're deciding whether to trust you. That's the moment they're closest to calling. Sending them elsewhere then is like walking a customer out of your showroom just as they reach for a pen.

Your ads pay for it

If the visitor came from Google Ads, you paid for that click. Every homeowner who leaves through a logo raises your real cost per lead.

It can dilute your SEO

A handful of outbound links to relevant, trusted sites is normal and fine. But pages that mostly push visitors away — and say little of their own — don't help Google understand why your page is the best answer for a roofing search in your town.

Keep the trust — keep the visitor

The answer isn't to hide your certifications. They're one of the things homeowners look for. The answer is to explain them on your own site.

  1. Make badges non-clickable, or link them to your page about that certification.
  2. Create a “Certifications & warranties” page on your site that explains, in plain words, what each certification means for the homeowner and what warranty they get when you install.
  3. Write your own shingle and material pages — the options you install, colors, pros and cons, with your own job photos — instead of sending people to brand brochures.
  4. If you must link out (for example, to let a homeowner verify a certification), open it in a new tab and put the link at the end of the section, after your call button.
  5. Put a call-to-action next to every trust signal: “Certified installer — book a free inspection.”

A 10-minute link audit

Open your website on a laptop and go page by page:

  • Hover every logo and badge. Where does it go?
  • Click every “learn more,” “warranty” and “brochure” link.
  • Note any link that leaves your site, especially in the header, the hero, the trust bar and the footer.
  • Check whether external links open in the same tab.
  • For each one, ask: does this help the homeowner call me? If not, change it.

If you use Google Analytics, you can also look at outbound clicks to see which links are actually sending people away.

What good looks like

On a well-built roofing site, the trust bar shows your Google rating, years in business, license and insurance, and your certifications — all on one line, none of them leaving the page. Each badge is explained on your own certifications page. Material pages show your jobs, in your towns. And every one of those pages ends the same way: a phone number one tap away and a short quote form.

What the homeowner is really asking

When a homeowner clicks a certification badge, they're not shopping for shingles. They're asking three questions: Is this roofer qualified? Will my roof be covered if something goes wrong? Is this company going to be around?

Your website can answer all three better than a manufacturer's page can, because you can talk about your crews, your warranty and your jobs in their town. A manufacturer's site will tell them about the product — and then help them find another installer.

So the job of every trust signal on your site is to answer one of those questions and then point to the next step: call, or request an inspection.

Trust-section checklist

  • Google rating and review count, linking to your own Google profile.
  • Years in business, and the owner's name.
  • License and insurance, with numbers where they exist.
  • Certifications you genuinely hold, explained on your own page.
  • Your workmanship warranty, in one plain sentence.
  • A call button within thumb's reach on mobile.

Other places roofing sites leak visitors

Manufacturer links are the most common leak, but not the only one:

  • Review widgets that open a third-party site full of other contractors' reviews. Show your Google reviews on your page and link to your own Google profile only.
  • Social icons in the header. A homeowner who taps your Facebook icon is now scrolling Facebook. Move social links to the footer.
  • Financing links that go straight to the lender's homepage. Link to your partner's application for your company, or explain financing on your own page first.
  • Directory badges (BBB, Angi, HomeAdvisor) linking to listing pages that show competitors next to you.
  • Blog posts that end without a next step. Every post should end with a clear way to contact you.

Example: rewriting a certification section

Before: a row of six brand logos under the heading “Our partners,” each linking to the manufacturer's homepage, with no text.

After: the heading “Certified installers — your warranty is backed twice.” Two or three badges you genuinely hold, each with one sentence on what it means for the homeowner (for example, which warranty options become available when a certified installer does the job). A link to your own “Certifications & warranties” page. And a button next to it: “Book a free inspection.”

The trust is the same — stronger, actually, because it's explained. But now the visitor stays on your site, closer to calling.

What to put on your certifications page

  • Each certification you hold, in plain words, and how a homeowner can verify it.
  • The warranty options you can offer, and what they cover — workmanship and materials, separately.
  • Photos of real jobs using those materials.
  • Answers to the warranty questions homeowners actually ask: is it transferable if they sell, what voids it, how to make a claim.
  • A phone number and a short form at the bottom.

The order to fix things in

  1. Header and hero first. Anything that leaves the site from the top of the homepage costs the most.
  2. The trust bar. Make badges non-clickable or point them to your own page.
  3. Service pages, especially roof replacement — the page with the highest-value visitors.
  4. Footer and blog posts last.

Most roofing sites can fix the worst leaks in an afternoon. Then watch your Google Analytics for a month: time on site and calls from the site should move in the right direction.

Quick answers

Should I remove manufacturer logos completely?

No. Genuine certifications are a real trust signal. Keep them; just stop them from sending people away, and explain them on your own page.

Is linking to manufacturers bad for SEO?

A few relevant outbound links are normal. The bigger problem is conversion: losing homeowners who were about to call. Fix that first.

Can I use a manufacturer's logo if I'm not certified?

Don't. Only show certifications and logos you've genuinely earned and are allowed to use — check the manufacturer's brand rules.
