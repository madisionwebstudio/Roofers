What homeowners look for before hiring a roofer (and how your website shows it)

Even referrals Google you first. Here are the ten things homeowners check before they call — and how to put every one of them on your website.

Ask a roofer where their best jobs come from and most will say referrals. That's true — but it hides something. Almost every referral still checks you online before they call. A neighbor gives them your name; they type it into Google; your website and your Google profile decide whether they pick up the phone.

So the real question isn't “do I need a good website?” It's “what are homeowners looking for when they land on it?” This guide walks through the ten things homeowners check before hiring a roofer, why each one matters, and exactly how your website should show it.

Why homeowners check so carefully

A roof is one of the biggest repairs a homeowner will ever pay for, and most only buy one or two in their lifetime. They don't know what a fair price looks like. They've heard stories about storm chasers who take a deposit and disappear. And they're often under pressure — a leak over the bedroom, shingles in the yard, an insurance adjuster on the calendar.

That combination — high cost, low experience, real fear of getting burned — makes homeowners careful. They're not browsing for fun. They're looking for reasons to trust you, or reasons to move on to the next roofer. Your website's job is to give them the first and remove the second.

The 10 things homeowners look for

1. Real photos of your work

Homeowners want proof you've done roofs like theirs. Stock photos of smiling models in hard hats do the opposite — they make a site look like a template and quietly lower trust. Real photos of your crew, your trucks and your finished jobs do more than any headline.

How your website shows it: a projects gallery organized by job type and town, before-and-after pairs, and real photos in the hero of each page. Ten good phone photos taken in decent light beat a hundred stock images.

2. Google reviews on the page

Most homeowners read reviews before choosing a local contractor [SOURCE NEEDED: local consumer review survey]. They'll find your Google reviews anyway — the question is whether your website shows them confidently or makes people go looking.

How your website shows it: your actual Google rating and recent reviews, pulled from your profile, near the top of the homepage and on every service page, with a link to see them all. Never write reviews yourself or copy them from elsewhere — the FTC's rule on fake reviews applies to businesses of every size [SOURCE NEEDED: FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials].

3. Licensed and insured, shown clearly

Homeowners worry about being liable if someone gets hurt on their roof, and about hiring someone who isn't legitimate. Licensing rules differ by state — some states license roofers, some don't [SOURCE NEEDED: your state's contractor licensing board]. Either way, homeowners want to see that you're properly insured and, where it applies, licensed.

How your website shows it: a clear “Licensed & insured” line near your phone number, with license numbers where they exist, and an About page that names the owner. If a homeowner can check it, show it.

4. Your service area and local cities

Homeowners want a local roofer — someone who'll still be around when the warranty matters. If your website doesn't say where you work, they'll assume you're not nearby.

How your website shows it: a service areas page listing the towns and suburbs you actually serve, with the state, and city pages for your most important areas — written with real local detail, not just the town name swapped in.

5. Storm and hail damage help

After a storm, homeowners have a lot of questions at once: Is my roof damaged? What should I photograph? Who do I call first? A roofer whose website answers those questions earns trust before the inspection even happens.

How your website shows it: a dedicated storm and hail damage page explaining what to look for, what to document, and how your free inspection works. Keep it current — an outdated storm page with an old date at the top tells homeowners nobody's home.

6. Insurance claim help

Many roof replacements involve an insurance claim, and homeowners find the process confusing. They want a roofer who'll explain it plainly and work with their adjuster.

How your website shows it: an insurance claims page that explains, step by step, how you help — without overpromising. Rules on what contractors can say and do around claims vary by state, so this page should be reviewed carefully [SOURCE NEEDED: your state's rules on contractors and insurance claims].

7. Financing options

A new roof is rarely a planned expense. If you offer financing, saying so clearly can be the difference between a homeowner calling you and putting the project off.

How your website shows it: a short financing section on the homepage and roof replacement page, with a link to your financing partner's application.

8. Warranties and manufacturer certifications

Homeowners want to know what happens if something goes wrong. A clear workmanship warranty and, if you have them, manufacturer certifications reassure them that you stand behind the work.

How your website shows it: plain-English warranty terms and the certification badges you've genuinely earned. Don't use a manufacturer's logo if you're not certified by them — and think about where those badges link (we cover that in this guide).

9. A fast, easy quote form

Some homeowners won't call — they'll fill in a form at 10pm. Every extra field you ask for gives them a reason to quit.

How your website shows it: a short form (name, phone, address, what's going on) on every page, and a thank-you page that tells them when you'll call back.

10. Your phone number one tap away

Most homeowners will find you on their phone. If they have to hunt for your number or copy it by hand, some won't bother.

How your website shows it: a tap-to-call number in the header and a sticky call button at the bottom of the screen on mobile. Test it yourself: open your site on your phone and tap the number.

Why this matters even for referrals

It's tempting to think referral customers have already decided. They haven't. A referral gets you onto the shortlist; your website decides whether you stay on it. A homeowner who gets your name from a neighbor and then finds an outdated site with no photos, no reviews and no clear phone number has every reason to call the second name on the list.

The same is true in reverse. A strong website makes referrals convert better, makes your Google Ads cheaper per lead (because more clicks turn into calls), and gives SEO something worth ranking.

Score your own website

Open your website on your phone and go through the list. For each of the ten, ask: can a homeowner see this within a few seconds, without hunting for it?

  • 8–10 yes: your website is doing its job. Focus on getting found — roofing SEO and Google Ads.
  • 5–7 yes: you're losing some calls. The missing items are usually photos, reviews and a storm page.
  • Under 5: your website is probably costing you jobs — including referrals.

Remember: they're on a phone

Most of these checks happen on a small screen, often in a driveway or standing next to a leak. Every item on the list has to be visible without zooming, pinching or hunting through menus. If your reviews are at the bottom of a long page, or your phone number is only in the footer, many homeowners will never see them.

Website mistakes that quietly break trust

Even roofers who have most of the ten items can lose homeowners to small things:

  • An old copyright year or a storm page from three seasons ago. It suggests the business might not be active.
  • Different phone numbers on the website, Google profile and truck wrap. Homeowners notice, and so does Google.
  • A contact form that silently fails. Send yourself a test every month and check the spam folder.
  • Pop-ups that cover the screen on a phone. One is plenty; make it easy to close.
  • Claims you can't back up. “#1 roofer in Texas” invites doubt. Specific, checkable facts — years in business, license numbers, real review counts — build trust instead.
