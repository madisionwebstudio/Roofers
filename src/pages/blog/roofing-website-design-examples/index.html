Example 1: A hero that answers “can I trust you?”

The first screen of a roofing website has one job: tell a homeowner, in about five seconds, that they're in the right place. The strongest examples do it with four things:

  • What you do and where: “Roof replacement and repair in Madison, WI and nearby towns.”
  • One reason to choose you: years in business, a warranty, a certification you actually hold — something specific.
  • One clear button: “Get a free inspection” or “Get a quote,” not three competing options.
  • A real photo: your crew or a finished roof, not a stock image of a smiling model.

Check yours: open your homepage on your phone. Without scrolling, can a stranger tell your town, your main service and how to contact you?

Example 2: Trust proof near the top

Roofing is a high-trust purchase: it's expensive, it's the house, and many homeowners have heard bad stories. Good sites put proof right under the hero instead of hiding it on an About page:

  • Star rating and number of Google reviews (linked to the real profile).
  • License and insurance details.
  • Manufacturer certifications — only the ones you hold.
  • Warranty terms in plain words.
  • Years in business and whether the owner is involved.

Example 3: One page per service

Weak roofing sites list services as bullet points on the homepage. Strong ones give each service its own page — replacement, repair, storm and hail damage, insurance claim help, metal roofing, gutters — with photos of that work, what the process looks like, typical questions and the same clear next step. These pages also give Google something specific to rank for each search.

Example 4: Real town pages

The best examples have a page for each main town they serve, and each page reads as if a local wrote it: neighborhoods, weather, typical roof types, permit notes, nearby projects and reviews. The weak version is thirty identical pages with the city name swapped — those rarely help and can drag the whole site down.

Example 5: A form that feels easy

Long forms lose people. The roofing sites that convert well usually ask for 4–6 things — name, phone, email, address or town, and what they need — and say what happens next (“We'll call within one business day”). Repeat the button down the page and keep it visible on phones with a sticky bar.

Example 6: Fast on a phone

Many homeowners look at roofing websites on mobile. Google's own research has long linked slower mobile pages to more people leaving [SOURCE NEEDED: cite Google/Think with Google mobile speed study]. Strong examples use compressed images, few scripts, readable text without zooming, and buttons big enough to tap.

Check yours: run your homepage through Google PageSpeed Insights on mobile. If the score is red or the page takes several seconds to show its main content, fix that before buying ads.

Example 7: Pages that answer real questions

Strong roofing websites have pages that answer what homeowners search before they're ready to call: how insurance claims work, what a roof inspection includes, metal versus shingle, how long a replacement takes, what financing options exist. These pages build trust, help SEO, and give your crew a link to send when they get the same question for the hundredth time.

Keep them honest: explain the process without promising what insurance will pay, and give ranges only when you can stand behind them.

Patterns to avoid

  • Auto-playing sliders that hide the message and slow the page.
  • Clickable manufacturer logos that send visitors to another website. See sending customers to shingle manufacturer websites.
  • Pop-ups the second the page loads, especially on phones.
  • Blocking pinch-zoom on mobile — it makes text hard to read for many people.
  • Stock photos of models in hard hats. Homeowners can tell.

A checklist to borrow

  • First screen: service, town, one reason to trust you, one button.
  • Proof right under the hero: reviews, license, insurance, warranty.
  • A page per service and per main town — each genuinely useful.
  • Real photos of your crew and your roofs.
  • A short form and a sticky contact bar on phones.
  • Fast on mobile; text readable without zooming.
  • Clear titles and descriptions on every page.

Quick answers

What makes a good roofing website?

A clear first screen (service, town, one button), proof near the top, a page per service and main town, real photos, a short form and fast loading on phones.

How many pages should a roofing website have?

Enough for each main service and main town, plus About, reviews, projects and contact. For many roofers that's somewhere between 10 and 25 pages. Quality matters more than count.

Should I show prices on my roofing website?

If you can give honest ranges or starting prices, it can filter out poor-fit leads. Many roofers prefer to explain what affects price and offer free inspections instead. Either can work.
